# Instructional deck design

Use this reference when the requested deck teaches adults through a recorded lesson, workshop, executive briefing, or course. Adapt it to the brief. These practices guide instructional design; they do not override the source evidence, an approved curriculum, or a brand system.

## Model and learning arc

- Establish one canonical model for the learning journey. Keep its labels, order, and visual grammar stable across the deck.
- Map diagnostics, competencies, examples, and secondary frameworks into that model. Do not introduce an unexplained second taxonomy that competes with it.
- Give each lesson a causal flow: tension or question, concept, mechanism, example, consequence, synthesis, and transition.
- Define a concept before using a case to illustrate it. Make the case test or reveal the concept instead of interrupting the argument.
- Demonstrate the method on a meaningful situation. When useful, include a worked example that shows inputs, reasoning, and output rather than only displaying a finished answer.
- Connect every slide to the previous and next slide. A visual or rhetorical motif may carry the transition, but the reasoning must remain explicit in the speaker notes.

## Adult learning

- Start from a recognizable decision, cost, aspiration, or problem. Explain why the idea matters before expanding the theory.
- Use prompts for prediction, comparison, diagnosis, and transfer. Let the learner form a view before revealing the explanation when that improves understanding.
- Distinguish a brief reflection prompt from a formal exercise. Reflections can occur inside a lesson; formal exercises should appear only where the learning design assigns them and should build toward the promised learner artifact.
- Show how the learner will use the concept at work or in life. Prefer observable decisions and behaviors over abstract encouragement.
- Keep visible slides focused. Put the presenter's explanation, facilitation cues, source nuance, likely learner responses, and transitions in editable presenter notes.

## Timing and proof of concept

- Estimate duration from the actual narration, pauses, questions, examples, and transitions. Slide count alone does not establish lesson length.
- For a proof of concept, define the exact sample before production: lesson or section covered, target duration, required notes, visual fidelity, and which interactions or exercises are represented.
- A proof of concept should demonstrate the intended content depth, storytelling, visual system, presenter voice, and instructional rhythm. Do not imply that one polished sample proves the entire course or deck has been produced.
- Validate the proof of concept with the same render, inspection, source, and editability checks that apply to the final deck.
